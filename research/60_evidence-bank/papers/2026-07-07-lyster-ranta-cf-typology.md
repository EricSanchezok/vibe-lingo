# Corrective Feedback and Learner Uptake

## Metadata

- Type: paper
- Date captured: 2026-07-07
- Source URL: https://doi.org/10.1017/s0272263197001034 (Studies in Second Language Acquisition, Cambridge)
- Authors: Roy Lyster (McGill University), Leila Ranta (Concordia University)
- Year: 1997
- Reliability: high (2,191 citations, foundational taxonomy, still dominant in CF research)
- Tags: sla, corrective-feedback, recasts, prompts, uptake

## Why It Matters

This is THE foundational study on corrective feedback typology in SLA. It established the six-type CF taxonomy (recasts, explicit correction, elicitation, clarification requests, metalinguistic feedback, repetition) that remains the standard framework 25+ years later. Its key finding — that recasts are the most common but least effective type — directly challenges the intuitive approach of "just correct the error." For VibeLingo's expression polish feature, this tells us which feedback format to prioritize.

## Key Claims

- Six types of corrective feedback were identified from 18.3 hours of immersion classroom transcripts.
- **Recasts** were overwhelmingly the most frequent (55% of all CF), but were least effective at eliciting student-generated repair.
- **Prompts** (elicitation, metalinguistic feedback, clarification requests, repetition) led to student-generated repair significantly more often.
- The distinction between **recasts** (teacher provides the correct form) and **prompts** (teacher pushes the student to self-correct) is the critical distinction for learning outcomes.
- Learner "uptake" (immediate response to feedback) is not the same as learning, but prompts create more opportunities for "negotiation of form."

## Evidence / Details

- Six CF types:
  1. **Explicit correction**: "No, you should say X."
  2. **Recast**: Teacher reformulates the error without explicitly marking it.
  3. **Clarification request**: "Pardon?" / "What do you mean?"
  4. **Metalinguistic feedback**: "It's masculine, not feminine." (comments on form without providing the answer)
  5. **Elicitation**: "How do we say X in the past tense?" (pauses for student to self-correct)
  6. **Repetition**: Teacher repeats the error with rising intonation to highlight it.
- Distribution: recasts 55%, elicitation 14%, metalinguistic 8%, clarification requests 11%, explicit correction 7%, repetition 5%.
- Uptake rates: prompts (elicitation, metalinguistic, clarification, repetition) → ~80-100% uptake with student repair; recasts → ~31% uptake, mostly simple repetition without repair.

## Implications for VibeLingo

- **Do NOT default to recasts**: The most "natural" correction (just rewriting the user's message correctly) is the least effective for learning. VibeLingo's polish feature should NOT simply fix the text silently.
- **Use prompts when possible**: Ask guiding questions, highlight what's wrong without immediately giving the answer, or use metalinguistic hints. Example: "This verb form doesn't match the subject — can you find the right one?"
- **Metalinguistic explanation has strong evidence**: The user's preference for "show me why" aligns with what works — metalinguistic feedback promotes learning.
- **Clarification requests work well but may feel unnatural in text**: "What do you mean?" works in speech but may confuse in a chat interface. Need to adapt.
- **Recasts have their place**: For beginners or high-cognitive-load situations, a quick recast + optional explanation may be the least disruptive approach.

## Limitations

- Classroom oral interaction study — may not fully generalize to text-based, one-on-one, agent-mediated interaction.
- Measured "uptake" (immediate repair), not long-term learning. Uptake ≠ acquisition.
- French immersion context — may not generalize to all L1-L2 pairs.
- Does not address written corrective feedback specifically.

## Follow-up Questions

- How do the six CF types map to text-based, asynchronous (chat) interaction?
- Is metalinguistic feedback more effective in writing (where the user has more time to process) than in speech?
- Does the agent's lack of human presence change the learner's receptiveness to different CF types?
