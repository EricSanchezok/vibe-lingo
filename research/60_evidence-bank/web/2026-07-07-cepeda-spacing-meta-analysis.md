## Why It Matters

Cepeda et al.'s meta-analyses are the definitive quantitative synthesis of the spacing effect — the finding that distributed practice produces dramatically better long-term retention than massed practice. The 2008 study specifically identifies optimal spacing intervals, which directly informs VibeLingo's review scheduling.

## Key Claims

- **Distributed practice dramatically improves long-term retention** compared to massed practice across all task types and retention intervals.
- **The optimal gap between study sessions is 10-20% of the desired retention interval**: To remember something for 1 year, space reviews ~1-2 months apart.
- **Longer gaps produce better long-term retention** (though with a cost to immediate performance).
- The spacing effect is one of the most robust findings in cognitive psychology.
- Effect is consistent across: verbal learning, motor skills, intellectual skills, various age groups.

## Evidence / Details

- 2006 meta-analysis: 317 experiments, comprehensive analysis of spacing and lag effects.
- 2008 study: optimal spacing gap ≈ 10-20% of retention interval.
  - For 1-week retention: gap of ~1 day is optimal.
  - For 1-year retention: gap of ~1-2 months.
- Spacing effect magnitude: typically Cohen's d = 0.5-1.0 (medium to large).
- The effect holds for both recall and recognition tests.
- The optimal spacing gap increases with the desired retention interval.

## Implications for VibeLingo

- **Reviews should be spaced, not massed**: Don't review all new vocabulary at once. Distribute.
- **Initial reviews can be close together**: For short-term retention (within a session), 1-day gaps work.
- **Long-term retention requires wider gaps**: To build durable vocabulary, reviews must be spaced weeks-months apart.
- **This supports FSRS/adaptive scheduling**: The optimal gap varies by item and user — static scheduling can't optimize.
- **Session-boundary reviews are valid**: Even reviews at "end of session" can contribute if they're the right spacing from the previous review.

## Limitations

- Lab studies, not real vocabulary learning in the wild.
- Does not address interaction with contextual factors (context richness, emotional engagement, etc.).
- Optimal gap is a population average; individual differences exist.
- Mostly verbal paired-associate learning; may not fully generalize to contextual vocabulary in agent sessions.

## Follow-up Questions

- How does the optimal spacing gap change when learning is incidental (not intentional memorization)?
- Does the spacing effect magnitude decrease when items are reviewed in context (agent session) vs. in isolation?
- What is the minimum number of reviews needed for durable vocabulary in the agent-session context?
